Epidemiology, Heterogeneous Nucleation & Presentation

Bladder stones account for 5% of all urinary stone disease and ~1.5% of urologic hospital admissions in Western countries, with a strong male predominance (4:1 to 10:1 per EAU Guidelines). In non-endemic regions they are predominantly secondary to stasis or foreign bodies in adults, whereas in endemic areas primary vesical calculi peak in boys aged 2–4 years (Campbell Ch. 100).

Pathogenesis & Composition

Driven by heterogeneous nucleation around infection, obstruction, or foreign bodies. >30% are infection-associated (urease-splitting bacteria); Takasaki found 52% contained struvite (MAP), while 7.7% of males vs. 0% of females had uric acid stones.

Clinical Presentation

Most common symptom is terminal hematuria paired with intermittency, frequency, urgency, weak stream, and suprapubic pain. Adults can present with acute urinary retention; children rarely present acutely.

Drug-Induced Bladder Calculi: Suspect triamterene (inhibits distal Na⁺ reabsorption in BOO patients with high post-void residuals) and indinavir in patients with radiolucent calculi or atypical crystal matrices (Campbell Ch. 100).
Clinical Controversy

Bladder Outlet Obstruction (BOO): Simultaneous vs. Staged BPE Surgery

Incomplete bladder emptying and failure to pass migratory fragments are present in 45%–79% of patients with vesical calculi (up to 88% in Douenias et al.), and a bladder stone occurs in ~2% of men undergoing TURP. However, urodynamic studies show only ~50% of some cohorts have true BOO—raising the question of whether concomitant TURP is always mandatory (Campbell Ch. 100; PMC8279246).

Case for Simultaneous TURP

2026 meta-analysis (>500 patients): Concomitant BPE + stone surgery slashed bladder stone recurrence (OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.10–0.33, p < 0.00001) and improved IPSS (−3.09 pts), while 10%–41% of stone-only patients later needed BPE surgery (PubMed 42503071).

Case for Stone-Only / Shared Decision

Multicenter cohort (PMC8279246): 81% of patients without TURP remained stone-free at 48 months (NNT = 7 for simultaneous TURP), with shorter hospital stay (1.6 vs. 3.4 days, p = 0.04) and fewer Clavien I–II complications.

Diagnostic Imaging Pearl: Supine and lateral decubitus bladder ultrasound (Fig. 100.2B–C) confirms mobile echogenic calculi with posterior acoustic shadowing—differentiating free stones from adherent tumors, clots, or encrusted sutures.

Bladder Stone Surgical Modalities (EAU Guidelines & Campbell Ch. 100)

Modality Key Evidence & Efficiency Best Indications Major Pitfalls / Risks
Transurethral Cystolithotripsy (TUCL) Using a continuous-flow nephroscope or resectoscope is significantly faster than a standard cystoscope across 5 RCTs; shorter hospital stay vs. PCCL. First-line for most adult stones < 3–4 cm with patent urethra; allows concurrent TURP/HoLEP. Urethral stricture risk 2.9%–19.6% at 12–24 mo; inefficient for massive burdens (>4 cm).
Percutaneous Cystolithotripsy (PCCL) Equal SFR to TUCL; enables large-bore Amplatz/dual-action ultrasonic-ballistic probes (16–30 Fr) with rapid fragment evacuation. Large stones, urethral strictures, closed bladder neck, continent catheterizable stomas, and pediatric boys. Irrigation fluid extravasation, loss of tract access in small trabeculated bladders, longer catheterization vs. TUCL.
Open Suprapubic Cystolithotomy Shortest operative time for massive stones; longest hospital stay and catheter duration. Very large / hard stones (>4–5 cm), concomitant open simple prostatectomy or large diverticulectomy. Wound infection, prolonged Foley, incisional morbidity.
SWL (Selected Cases) Lower 1-session SFR (86% vs. 98% TUCL, p = 0.03), reaching 94% after up to 3 sessions for stones ≤ 2 cm; shortest LOS. High anesthetic-risk / SCI patients prone to autonomic dysreflexia with small stones. Requires patent outlet for fragment passage; repeated sessions often needed.

0.3%–1% of Urinary Stones

Urethral & Preputial Calculi

Urethral calculi represent 0.3%–1% of all urinary stones and occur far more commonly in males. Posterior urethral stones account for up to 88% vs. 4%–11% in the anterior urethra (Campbell Ch. 100).

Migratory vs. Primary/Diverticular: Migratory stones are solitary and present with acute painful urinary retention (preceded by flank pain 1–2 weeks earlier). Primary stones form behind strictures or inside urethral diverticula (palpable in perineum in males or anterior vaginal wall in females) with insidious LUTS.
Endoscopic Management ("Push-Back" vs. In-Situ Laser): For posterior urethral stones without stricture, gently push the stone retrograde into the bladder ("push-back") for safe cystolitholapaxy. For firmly impacted bulbomembranous or anterior stones (Fig. 100.3), perform in-situ Ho:YAG laser fragmentation—never pull forcefully with baskets or forceps against urethral mucosa!
Preputial Calculi: Associated with severe phimosis, poor hygiene, and struvite (MAP) composition; plain X-ray detects 14%. Treat with dorsal slit or circumcision and send excised prepuce for histopathology to exclude underlying squamous carcinoma.
Prostatic & Ejaculatory Tract

True Prostatic Calculi & Seminal Vesicle Stones

Prostatic Calculi

Seen in up to 99% of adult males on autopsy. Endogenous (within acini) vs. exogenous (urine reflux—fewer, larger). Composition: 83% calcium phosphate, 8.7% carbonate phosphate, 4.5% CaOx.

Men with prostatitis/CPPS are 3.2× more likely to harbor significant prostatic calculi (and positive E. coli/Klebsiella). No link to prostate cancer. Remove exposed stones with the endo-loop during TURP for better symptom relief.

Seminal Vesicle (SV) Calculi

Present with hematospermia, painful ejaculation, low ejaculate volume, or perineal pain. Composed of CaP, struvite, or protein matrix.

TRUS is most accurate; MRI shows T2 hypointensity without diffusion restriction. Transurethral seminal vesiculoscopy (TRU-SVS) using a 6-Fr vesiculoscope via the verumontanum orifice + holmium laser achieves clearance with fewer complications than TURED.

Intraoperative Safety Checklist (TUCL & PCCL)

  • Continuous-Flow Instrument Selection: Use a resectoscope sheath or continuous-flow nephroscope transurethrally rather than a narrow cystoscope to double clearance speed and prevent bladder overdistension.
  • Avoid "Wall Pinning" Perforation: Pinning mobile fragments against a thin, trabeculated bladder wall with an ultrasonic or ballistic probe risks mechanical/thermal perforation; use suction-assisted evacuation or laser dusting in low-distension pockets.
  • Prevent PCCL Tract Loss: Keep a safety guidewire inside the bladder throughout PCCL; small trabeculated bladders easily slip off short trocars or Amplatz sheaths, causing extraperitoneal irrigant extravasation.
  • Post-Op Cystogram Threshold: If significant mucosal trauma, deep laser divot, or suspected intraperitoneal extravasation occurs during PCCL/TUCL, perform a low-pressure cystogram prior to catheter removal (CJU PCCL Series).
