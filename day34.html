Photomechanical vs. Photothermal Lithotripsy

Pulse duration determines how laser energy shatters calculi (Campbell Ch. 19). Ultrashort pulses (< 500 ns, Q-switched) exceed $10^8\text{ W/cm}^2$ irradiance, generating plasma and spherical cavitation bubbles (>300 bars) that fracture stones photomechanically. In contrast, modern mid-infrared Ho:YAG, TFL, and pulsed Tm:YAG lasers operate at pulse durations of hundreds of microseconds (> 250 µs)—producing asymmetric bubbles with weak pressure transients (8–20 bars at < 1 J) and fragmenting stones primarily via photothermal vaporization and interstitial water microexplosion.

The "Moses" Vapor Channel

Mid-infrared energy is absorbed far more strongly by liquid water than water vapor. During long-pulse Ho:YAG, the first ~60 µs vaporizes intervening water, opening a vapor tunnel that transmits the remainder of the pulse cleanly to the stone.

20-Millisecond Collateral Limit

Pulses longer than 20 ms (or continuous-wave lasers) conduct excessive heat into surrounding urothelium, causing coagulation and carbonization. Keep lithotripsy pulse widths well below 20 ms.

Intraoperative Safety

Laser Thermal Load & Power Calculator

Average power ($P = E \times f$) and Operator Duty Cycle ($\text{ODC}$) determine heat delivered to the collecting system. Both Ho:YAG and TFL deposit identical thermal energy per watt (PMC10693507).

Key Thermal Benchmarks (43 °C Threshold)

  • Gravity Irrigation Trap (Noureldin et al.): In live porcine RIRS under gravity irrigation (1 m height) without a UAS, just 20 W for 20 seconds drove intrarenal temperature to 54 °C—far above the 43 °C cytotoxic threshold (PMC10693507).
  • Pedal Activation Time (Aldoukhi et al.): At 40 W (50% duty cycle), the 43 °C injury threshold was crossed in just 9 seconds. Use short 3–5 s bursts with equal pauses.
  • Protective Value of UAS + Flow: Using a UAS lowered temperature rise from 5.8 °C/min to 3.8 °C/min (Gallegos et al.), and in a 2025 fragmenting model (16 W), irrigation + 10/12 Fr UAS capped peak temp at 42.9 °C vs. 52 °C without flow (PMC12387338).
  • High-Flow Target for ≥ 30 W: MRI thermometry indicates ≥ 70 mL/min irrigation is needed to stay below 120 min CEM43 when firing 30 W for 10 s (Wriedt et al.).
Electrical Principles

Electrosurgery vs. Electrocautery & Waveforms

Applying Ohm’s Law ($V = I \times R$; AC impedance $Z$), electrosurgery passes high-frequency AC current directly through patient tissue to a dispersive return pad (monopolar) or between twin forceps jaws (bipolar). By contrast, true electrocautery heats a metallic loop directly with current—the patient is not part of the circuit (Campbell Ch. 19).

Cut Mode (50–80 W)

Continuous waveform, lower voltage, high current. Rapidly boils intracellular water for clean vaporization with minimal thermal spread/char.

Coag Mode (30–50 W)

Interrupted 30,000×/s (~6% duty cycle), higher voltage, lower current. Heat disperses up to 3 mm; spray mode ionizes a spark gap without tissue contact.

Bipolar vs. Monopolar TURP: Bipolar circuits eliminate return through the patient and permit 0.9% normal saline irrigation—dramatically lowering dilutional hyponatremia and TUR syndrome (M-TURP Na⁺ drop 6.4 vs. 4.3 mmol/L, RR 0.17; PMC11436255).

Electrosurgical Safety, Implants & Specialized RF

Dispersive Pad & Prostheses

Surgical electrosurgical injury occurs in 0.1%–0.5% of cases. Never place dispersive pads over bony prominences, scars, hair, pacemakers, or orthopedic implants (place contralateral to a hip prosthesis).

Surgical Fire Hazard

550–650 US surgical fires occur annually, and electrosurgery is implicated in 68%—most involving alcohol prep pooling or supplemental O₂ near the upper torso/head.

EMDA & Radiofrequency (RFA)

EMDA uses an electric field (iontophoresis/electroporation) to boost bladder Mitomycin C uptake. RFA (375–900 kHz) induces ionic Joule heating within tissue, targeting 60 °C for renal tumor cell death.

Acoustic Lithotripsy

Shock Wave (SWL) vs. Burst Wave Lithotripsy (BWL)

Traditional SWL delivers broadband, high-peak-pressure shockwaves followed by a large negative tensile wave ($P_-$) that creates massive cavitation bubble clouds—requiring slower rates (60–80 shocks/min) to avoid acoustic shielding and general/sedation anesthesia. Burst Wave Lithotripsy (BWL) delivers narrowband ultrasound bursts (5–100 cycles, 200–1,000 kHz) at much lower peak pressures (5–8 MPa) under real-time ultrasound guidance (Campbell Ch. 19).

16× Faster Bubble Dissolution: Because BWL’s negative pressure phase is weaker and shorter, cavitation bubbles do not grow large and dissolve roughly 1/16th as fast—enabling high burst rates (≥ 10 Hz) without acoustic shielding.
Awake / Unanesthetized Treatment: Minimal cavitation cloud formation plus lower peak pressure allows BWL to be performed in the clinic or emergency department without anesthesia; operators pause only if a dynamic white cavitation cloud appears on ultrasound.
